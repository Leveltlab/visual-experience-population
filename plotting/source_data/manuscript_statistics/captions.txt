Figure caption

Orientation coding and contrast-dependent expansion of visual population responses.

(A) Schematic distinction between grating direction and orientation. Opposite motion directions share the same orientation; population responses can therefore distinguish eight directions or cluster into four orientation representations.

(B) Cosine similarity between centered population-response centroids for opposite (180°) and orthogonal (90°) directions, pooling the two highest contrasts (approximately 52–100%). Gray and black guides indicate idealized orientation and direction codes, respectively.

(C) Cross-validated linear discriminant analysis (LDA) accuracy for four orientations (solid bars) and eight directions (hatched bars), using the same high-contrast pool. Dashed and dotted lines indicate 25% and 12.5% chance accuracy, respectively.

(D) Three-dimensional principal-component projections of the eight direction centroids in each area, using a shared PCA frame after rigid alignment. Filled markers denote 0°–135° and open, dashed-outline markers their opposite directions. Area-specific magnification is indicated by the scale bars. Insets show squared population radius, r², defined as the mean squared distance of the eight direction centroids from their center in the full ten-neuron space, rather than in the PCA projection.

(E) Schematic illustration of collapsed versus expanded orientation representations and the four adjacent contrast pools used for transfer decoding.

(F) Peak squared orientation radius for the bottom-two (approximately 1–2%) and top-two (approximately 52–100%) contrast pools, shown on a logarithmic ordinate. Opposite-direction trials are pooled into four orientation centroids. The peak over 0–0.5 s is taken after averaging neuron subsamples and sessions within each mouse.

(G) Orientation-decoding transfer from higher to lower adjacent contrast pools: 4→3, 3→2 and 2→1. The four pools are approximately 1–2%, 3.7–7.2%, 14–27% and 52–100%. The classifier is trained on the higher-contrast pool and evaluated on held-out trials from the lower-contrast pool; the dashed line marks 25% chance.

(H) Contrast-dependent population geometry in AM–PM and A–AL–RL at baseline and chronic recordings. Here, low and high pools contain the bottom four (approximately 1–7.2%) and top four (approximately 14–100%) contrasts. All eight directions, four conditions and four areas enter alignment and the shared PCA; opposite-direction coordinates are averaged only afterward for display. Small faint filled markers denote low contrast and larger dark open markers high contrast; arrows connect matched centroids and outlines join high-contrast centroids. All four views share a camera and scale bar.

Colors identify dark-reared (DR) and normally reared (NR) mice at baseline and chronic recordings; circles and squares denote DR and NR, respectively. Analyses use raw deconvolved activity and 1,000 ten-neuron subsamples per session–area population. Responses are averaged over 0–0.5 s except for the time-resolved peak calculation in F. In B, C, D insets, F and G, points/bars show equally weighted mouse means after within-mouse session averaging; error bars indicate pointwise 95% mouse-bootstrap confidence intervals. PCA panels are descriptive group-mean visualizations.

Statistical caption

(B, C, F, G) Four-factor nonparametric longitudinal ANOVA-type tests (nparLD 2.3.3; unweighted pseudo-ranks; 27 mice: 16 DR, 11 NR). Rearing is between mice; area, time and the panel-specific factor are repeated within mice. Incomplete records are retained without imputation. Each main effect averages equally over the other factors. C uses chance-normalized accuracy, (accuracy − chance)/(1 − chance); F uses log peak r². ATS denotes the ANOVA-type statistic, evaluated against an approximate F(df, ∞) distribution. Primary effects: B, direction difference, ATS = 18960.94, df = 1; C, decoder task, ATS = 235.91, df = 1; F, contrast, ATS = 1185.70, df = 1; G, all three contrast transitions, ATS = 435.54, df = 1.92; all adjusted p < 0.0001. Holm correction is applied separately within each panel across its four main effects (panel-specific factor, area, rearing and time). No correction is applied across panels.

Other significant main effects, using the same within-panel correction: rearing in B (ATS = 19.28, df = 1, p < 0.0001), C (ATS = 18.59, df = 1, p < 0.0001) and G (ATS = 34.81, df = 1, p < 0.0001); area in C (ATS = 7.71, df = 2.58, p = 0.000213) and G (ATS = 4.77, df = 2.80, p = 0.00641); and time in G (ATS = 4.76, df = 1, p = 0.0292). All other main effects were nonsignificant after correction. ANOVA-type p values are asymptotic; inference assumes noninformative missing coverage (missing completely at random) and remains limited by cells with as few as two mice.

(B, C, D inset, F, G) NR versus DR: two-sided exact Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann-Whitney U) tests, Holm-corrected for the two timepoints separately within each area and endpoint for B–F. G uses Holm correction across all six comparisons (two timepoints × three transitions) within each area. Endpoints were 180° similarity (B), orientation accuracy (C), r² (D), log(high/low) peak r² (F), and each of the three transfer accuracies (G). Significant baseline comparisons: V1 in B and G 3→2; A–AL–RL in B, C, D, F and G 4→3/3→2. No G 2→1 comparison survived correction. No chronic comparison survived correction. Group sizes (NR/DR, baseline; chronic): V1, 9/10; 8/6; AM–PM, 5/4; 3/4; A–AL–RL, 8/13; 4/10; LM, 3/6; 2/4 mice. Main-effect stars denote corrected nonparametric ANOVA results; brackets denote corrected NR–DR comparisons. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
